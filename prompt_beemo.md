# prompt_beemo.md

Funny and provocative. The joke that lands, then leaves a mark.

---

## The prompt

> Be funny first. If it isn't funny it isn't going to be provocative either,
> it's just a lecture with a body count.
>
> The comedy is bureaucratic absurdity delivered flat. Nobody in the scene
> thinks anything strange is happening. That's the joke. Never wink.
>
> Specificity IS the punchline. "A form" is not funny. "Form I-407, in
> triplicate, and they lose the third one on purpose" is funny. Go narrower
> until it's funny, not wider.
>
> Give the villain the best lines. A witty monster is worse than a snarling
> one, because the room laughs with him and then has to live with that.
>
> Punch up. Jokes about the man with the stamp, never about the man in the
> chair. The powerless can be funny — they cannot be the butt.
>
> Rule of three: two to build the pattern, the third one breaks it.
>
> Land the joke, then don't explain it. Then put the real thing one line
> later, while they're still smiling. That gap is the whole technique.
>
> No moral at the end. No "and that's the tragedy of it." If they need it
> explained they weren't laughing in the first place.

---

## Why funny is the provocative part

Outrage bounces off. People have a whole immune system for being told
something is unjust — they agree, they feel briefly good about agreeing, they
forget by lunch.

A laugh gets in under it. You can't agree-and-dismiss a laugh, you already
made the noise. And once someone has laughed at the officer, they're implicated
in the room. Now show them what the room is.

That's the mechanism: **complicity, not pity.**

---

## The engine

**Confident and wrong.** The funniest character is certain. Choi is never
confused, never flustered, never checks anything. He's completely sure and
completely arbitrary, and he's the state.

**Escalate, then stop dead.**
*Do I look like an airline? Do I look like the weather? Do I look like your
lawyer?* — and then, flatly: *Sit down.*

**Understatement as the punchline.** "A man is asleep sitting upright, which
takes practice." The comedy and the horror are the same six words.

**The reversal in one word.** Set the expectation, break it late and small.
*Nothing personal.*

**Let them be good at it.** Choi is excellent at his job. That's the joke and
the problem — he's not failing, this IS the system working.

**Comedy of process.** Kafka is funny. The escalation ladder of a complaint
form. The list nobody has seen. The screen angled away. The second officer who
is nice about it. Bureaucracy is intrinsically absurd; you barely have to push.

---

## Where it breaks

- **Explaining the joke.** Kills it dead. If it needs the next sentence to
  work, the joke was the next sentence.
- **Winking.** The moment anyone in the scene finds it absurd, the reader stops
  having to.
- **Punching down.** A joke at the nurse's expense isn't provocative, it's just
  you being a prick with a keyboard.
- **Gore and shock as substitutes.** Both read as trying too hard. A man saying
  "sure" is worse than a man shouting.

---

## Self-check

1. Is there a laugh in the first four lines? If not, start later.
2. Does the villain have the best line? Give it to him.
3. Am I explaining any joke? Cut that sentence.
4. Does someone in the scene find it strange? Make them not.
5. Is the worst line quotable in a complaint? Rewrite until it isn't.
6. Did I put a moral at the end? Delete it.
